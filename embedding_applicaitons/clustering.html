Clustering: finding groups without labels

Nobody tells the computer what the groups are. It looks at the data and discovers them itself.

What is clustering → algorithms on plain numbers (K-means, DBSCAN, hierarchical) → clustering text with embeddings

1. What is clustering?

Everyday picture: you pour a bag of mixed laundry on the floor and sort it into piles: whites, darks, towels. Nobody gave you the pile names in advance. You made piles by putting similar things together.
  • Other examples: customer segments, grouping news articles by topic, finding similar songs, spotting unusual transactions.
  • It is unsupervised learning: there is no "right answer" column, unlike classification.
  • Two things you must decide: how to measure "similar" (distance) and what a group looks like (round blob? any shape?). Different algorithms answer these differently.

2. Algorithms on plain numbers (no embeddings)

When every item is already a few numbers (age, income, x/y position), we can measure similarity with ordinary distance and cluster directly. Here are three classics. Play with them on the same data.

K-means

  1. Choose k. Drop k centroids (the X marks) on random data points.
  2. Assign: every point joins its nearest centroid.
  3. Update: move each centroid to the average of its points.
  4. Repeat 2 and 3 until nothing changes.
minimise inertia = sum over points of distance(point, its centroid)^2
+ simple, fast, works on millions of points.   − you must pick k; assumes round, similar-size blobs; sensitive to the random start; outliers drag centroids.

How to pick k? Inertia always falls as k grows, so look for the "elbow" where the improvement slows down:

DBSCAN (density based)

  1. A point with at least minPts neighbours within distance eps is a core point.
  2. Core points that are neighbours join the same cluster; the cluster keeps growing through core points.
  3. Points that belong to no dense area are labelled noise (grey).
+ no k needed; finds any shape (try the moons); detects outliers.   − you must tune eps/minPts; struggles when densities differ.

Hierarchical (agglomerative)

  1. Start with every point as its own cluster.
  2. Repeatedly merge the two closest clusters.
  3. Stop when k clusters are left (or draw the full tree, a dendrogram).
"Closest" depends on the linkage: single = distance between the nearest two points (can follow a chain, good for moons), average = average of all pairwise distances (prefers round groups, like K-means).
+ no random start, gives a full tree.   − slow on big data (about O(n²) or worse).
The limitation of everything above

These algorithms need each item to be a short list of meaningful numbers. What are the "coordinates" of the sentence "my puppy loves the park"? Counting words gives huge, mostly-zero vectors in which puppy and dog are as different as puppy and pizza. We need numbers that capture meaning. That is what embeddings give us.

3. Clustering with embeddings

Recipe, the same one you used for semantic search:

text --embedding model--> vector --clustering algorithm--> groups of similar meaning "my cat loves the puppy" -> [0.9, 0.0, 0.0, 0.0] -> cluster "pets"
  • The clustering algorithm is the same (K-means, DBSCAN, hierarchical). Only the input changed: vectors from a model such as all-MiniLM-L6-v2 (384 numbers).
  • Distance between embeddings is usually cosine. If vectors are normalised to length 1, ordinary K-means works well.
  • Uses: group support tickets by issue, find topics in reviews, de-duplicate similar articles, label unlabeled data.

Choosing and checking clusters on text

  • k too small merges different topics; k too large splits one topic. Try k = 2 and k = 6 above.
  • Always read samples from each cluster and name them. If you cannot name a cluster, it probably is not meaningful.
  • For high-dimensional embeddings, reduce to 2-D with PCA or UMAP only for plotting.
